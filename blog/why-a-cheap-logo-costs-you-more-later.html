Why That $200 Logo From Fiverr Will Cost You More Later

September 2026 · Paul Danowski

A $200 logo isn't a bad deal because it's cheap. It's a bad deal because of what's missing from the file you get back — and you usually don't find out what's missing until you need it.

If you've ever posted a logo job on a marketplace and picked a design off a grid of nearly identical submissions, you already got what you paid for: a picture. What you didn't get is what a logo is actually supposed to do for a business, and that gap is what ends up costing you.

01You don't actually own a working file — you own a picture

Most marketplace logo deliveries hand you a PNG or a JPEG: a flattened image, not the vector file (.AI, .EPS, .SVG) it was built in. That's fine on Instagram. It's not fine the first time you need it on a vehicle wrap, a storefront sign, a shirt print, or a trade show banner, where it has to scale to eight feet wide without turning into mush. Getting it redrawn from scratch, because there's no source file, is a second logo bill you didn't budget for.

02It only works at the one size it was designed for

A logo has to work as a 16-pixel favicon, a black-and-white fax stamp, a single-color embroidery patch, and a 40-foot sign — often all in the same month. A cheap mark is usually built once, for one placement, with fine detail and gradients that disappear the moment it's shrunk or printed in one color. If your logo turns into a smudge on a business card, that's not a printing problem.

03It probably isn't only yours

Marketplace and "logo generator" designs draw from the same small pool of shapes and stock icon packs — which is exactly why they're fast and cheap. It's common for the same mark, or something close enough to notice, to show up on a competitor's site, a different city, a different industry entirely. A logo's whole job is to be recognizably, specifically yours. One that isn't can't do that job.

04There's no one to call when you need something changed

A month in, you need a horizontal version for the website header, an icon-only version for the app, a white version for the dark background on the truck wrap. With a marketplace job, that designer is gone — on to the next $200 gig — and you're either paying a stranger to reverse-engineer someone else's file, or living with a logo that only fits the one place it was first used.

05You'll pay for a real one eventually, so you're paying twice

Almost every business that starts with a marketplace logo replaces it within a couple of years — usually right as the business is growing and a real identity actually starts to matter. The $200 was never the cost of a logo. It was a down payment on the one you'll commission later, on top of what you already spent on the first one.

A logo is the cheapest part of a rebrand — the redo is what's expensive

The actual cost of a logo was never the invoice. It's the source files you can hand off, the variations that hold up at any size, and having someone who already understands your business to call when the brand needs to grow into something new. Skip the version that has to be replaced, and go straight to the one that doesn't.
