Five Things That Kill a Local Business Website

September 2026 · Paul Danowski

Most local business websites don't fail because they're ugly. They fail because they make a visitor work too hard for basic information, on the one device that visitor is actually using.

If you run a bar, a restaurant, or any business people find on their phone before they walk in the door, here are the five most common ways your website is quietly costing you customers — and what to actually do about each one.

01It doesn't say what you do, or where you are, above the fold

A visitor lands from a Google search or a friend's text with one question: is this the place, and is it open right now. If the homepage opens with a full-bleed photo and no name, address, or hours in the first screen, that visitor is already checking the next tab. Put your name, your neighborhood, and your hours where nobody has to scroll to find them.

02It's not built for a phone

The person deciding whether to walk in is standing outside with a phone in hand — not sitting at a desktop. If the menu requires pinch-zooming, if buttons are too small to tap, if the layout breaks on a six-inch screen, you've lost them before they've read a word about your food. Mobile isn't a "version" of the site. For a bar or restaurant, it is the site.

03The menu is a PDF — or a photo of a printed menu

A PDF menu loads slowly, fights the phone's browser, and Google can't read it — so your dishes don't show up in search at all. A photo of a laminated menu is worse: often unreadable, and the first thing to go stale the moment a price changes. Menus should be real text on a real page — fast, searchable, and easy to update the day the kitchen changes something.

04There's no obvious next step

A good local site earns the click. It also has to make it dead simple to act on it. If "call us," "get directions," and "reserve a table" aren't one tap away — no click-to-call, no map link, a phone number buried in a footer — you're asking a hungry, impatient visitor to do work that should take zero seconds.

05It hasn't been touched in years, and looks it

A copyright year from three presidents ago, a layout that breaks, a "coming soon" banner from a launch that already happened — these tell a visitor something worse than "outdated." They say "we might not still be doing this." Trust gets decided in seconds. A site that looks abandoned makes people assume the business might be too.

None of this needs a full rebuild

Most of it is a focused stretch of work: real information where it belongs, a menu that's actually a webpage, and a site that works as hard on a phone as it does on a laptop. If any of the five above sound familiar, that's the sign — fix what's actually costing you customers, not everything at once.

If your site is doing any of this, let's fix it — not with a redesign marathon, with the parts that are actually losing you business.
