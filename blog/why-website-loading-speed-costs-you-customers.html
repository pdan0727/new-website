Why Your Website’s Loading Speed Is Costing You Customers

September 2026 · Paul Danowski

You’ve got three seconds. That’s roughly how long a mobile visitor gives your website before deciding whether to stay or bail — and for a lot of local business sites, three seconds isn’t nearly enough time to even load.

Tenths of a second show up in revenue

It sounds dramatic until you look at the numbers. Portent’s analysis of over 100 million page views found that a site loading in one second converts visitors at nearly three times the rate of a site that takes five seconds. On the lead-generation side, that gap is even wider — a one-second site converts roughly three to five times better than a slow one. Google and Deloitte ran a joint study across 37 brands and found that shaving just one-tenth of a second off load time raised retail conversions by 8.4% and travel-site conversions by over 10%. Not one second — one-tenth of one second. That’s how sensitive people are to delay, even when they don’t consciously notice it.

The bar most local sites aren’t clearing

Here’s the part that should get any local business owner’s attention: most small business websites are failing this test right now. Recent Chrome data shows only about 48% of mobile pages pass Google’s core speed benchmarks — meaning the majority of small business sites are loading slower than what search engines consider “good.” And the platform matters more than people realize: sites built on WordPress pass at under half the rate of sites built on more modern, streamlined platforms. If your site was built years ago, patched together with plugins, or never optimized for mobile, there’s a good chance it’s quietly turning away the exact customers your marketing is working to bring in.

A slow site is harder to find, too

Speed also affects whether people find you at all. Google has confirmed that page speed is part of how it ranks search results — not the single biggest factor, but often the tiebreaker between you and the competitor down the street when your content is otherwise similar. And since mobile makes up roughly half or more of all web traffic, a site that’s slow on phones is losing ground twice: once in the search rankings, and again when the visitor actually lands on the page and leaves before it finishes loading.

What actually makes a site slow, in plain terms

  • Oversized images that were never compressed for the web
  • Bloated themes and plugins doing more work than the site actually needs
  • No caching, so the server rebuilds the same page from scratch for every visitor
  • Fonts, scripts, and trackers loaded from half a dozen different outside sources
  • Hosting that was never built to handle real traffic

None of these are exotic problems. They’re also not things most business owners have the time, tools, or reason to know how to diagnose — running a restaurant, a shop, or a service business is a full-time job on its own. But they’re fixable, usually without rebuilding a site from the ground up.

Find out where you stand

If you’re not sure where your own site stands, Google’s free PageSpeed Insights tool will give you a score in about ten seconds. If that number is lower than you’d like — or if you already suspect your site feels sluggish on your phone — it might be worth having someone take a real look under the hood rather than guessing. That’s exactly the kind of audit I do for local businesses: find out what’s actually slowing the site down, fix what’s fixable, and make sure the site isn’t quietly costing you customers before they ever see what you offer.
